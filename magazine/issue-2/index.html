Welcome back to TAPPAS.

This issue has two headline stories. The first is the one we promised at the end of Issue 01: we go inside KE:SAI, the Tübingen non-profit that wants to build the Linux of self-driving, with no investors, no equity and no patents, and that plans to give away everything it builds.

The second is about a question many of us think about and rarely talk about at work: whether, and when, to have children. This autumn we asked, and 47 of you answered. The results are in By the Numbers. Most of us who don't have children would like to. Most of those are waiting. And almost nobody knows what our workplaces actually offer. Alongside the numbers, People talks to parents in Tübingen's AI labs about what really changes.

Elsewhere, FridayTalks rounds up a quarter of talks. And since it is winter, Fynn has sorted the region's Christmas markets so you can pick the right one, and the right night, and Rabanus has the dates.

As before, everything here was written, surveyed and photographed by people from our community, and we would love more of you on these pages: a piece, an interview, a photo, a rant. Find us in #tappas on Slack, or through the feedback form at the back.

— Haiwen & Mina, for the editorial team

Survey · Parenthood and AI careers

The Wheel Never Stops

Whether and when to have children is something many of us think about and rarely talk about at work. So we asked, and 47 of you answered. Most of those without children want them. Most of those are waiting. And almost nobody knows what their workplace offers.

By Haiwen Huang & Mina Remeli ·

At the start of November we sent round a short survey, Parenthood and AI Careers. It asked parents what changed and what helped. It asked everyone else whether they want children and what holds them back. And it asked all of us what our workplaces offer, and what this community could do.

Forty-seven of you answered: nine parents (one of them expecting), 37 people without children, and one person who preferred not to say. As with the flying survey in Issue 01, this is not a representative sample. People who care about the topic are more likely to answer, and nine parents are too few to generalise from. That is why we give counts rather than percentages. But the pattern in the answers is clear enough to be worth reading.

Thank you to everyone who took the five minutes, and especially to those who wrote something in the free-text boxes. A selection of those answers closes this piece.

The survey, in brief

47 answers from the Tübingen AI community, collected between late October and 10 November 2026. Anonymous, voluntary, and not representative. 27 women and 20 men answered.

Wanting, and waiting

Of the 37 respondents without children, 18 said they would like to have children someday and 14 that they might. Four said no, and three of those four added that their career has nothing to do with it. That is worth saying out loud: not wanting children is a complete answer, not a symptom of a bad job.

For the other 32, the career is very much part of it. Seventeen said their academic career has made them postpone, or think about postponing, having children significantly; eleven somewhat. Only one said no.

The difference between women and men is one of degree, not direction. Almost everyone who wants children has felt the pull to wait: 18 of 21 women, and 10 of 11 men. But 13 of the 21 women said significantly, against 4 of the 11 men.

“Right now it looks like the wheel never stops.”
— from the survey's free-text answers

What holds us back

We asked those 32 to pick their top three concerns. The answers are about the shape of an academic career far more than about babies. Falling behind on publications came first (15 of the 31 who answered), then fixed-term contracts and having to move for the next position (14), and salary (12). Age and biological timing also drew 12, and here the gender gap is wide: 10 of the 20 women who answered picked it, against 2 of the 11 men.

One option nobody picked is worth noting. Not a single person chose “how supervisors or colleagues would react”. Finding childcare was picked once, though the parents, as we will see, tell a different story. The worry, in other words, is not the reaction in the room. It is the contract, the CV and the clock.

“The need to publish and have a good career, so as to have stability and the possibility of having kids — which itself makes the ‘falling behind’ point happen.”
— a concern, in one respondent's own words

What the parents told us

Nine respondents are parents or expecting. Four became parents during their PhD, three as postdocs (one of them is still expecting) and two outside academia. For their most recent child they took, or plan to take, anything from no time off to a year: one took no leave, two less than two months, three between two and six months, and three between six and twelve.

Asked how supported they felt, the parents gave their supervisors and colleagues mostly good marks; five felt “very” supported by their supervisor. Their institutions got a lukewarm “fairly” from seven of the nine. Childcare split the group: four felt very supported by it, two only a little or not at all. One parent wrote that on-site and emergency childcare is “not really existent”.

And almost everything changed. Seven parents answered our checklist of what has changed since becoming a parent (one answered an earlier version of the form, and one is still expecting). All seven ticked when and how they organise their work, and how much they travel to conferences. Six ticked how many hours they work and what they prioritise. Four ticked their career plans, including whether to stay in academia. Nobody ticked “nothing major has changed”.

The most common answer: “Don't know”

We asked everyone whether, as far as they know, their workplace offers five kinds of support. Only one is widely known: 32 of 47 said they can get flexible hours or remote work. For everything else “don't know” dominated: 25 for Kita places, 31 for a family office or contact point, 35 for emergency childcare, and 36 for contract extensions. Eight people answered “don't know” to all five.

Part of this is structural. Tübingen's AI community works for many employers (the university, Max Planck, ELLIS, Hertie, companies), each with its own offers, which is also why the same question drew both “yes” and “no”: eleven of each for Kita places. But it is not just newcomers who don't know. Eight of the nine parents did not know whether fixed-term contracts can be extended for parents. That is the question with the most at stake, for a community in which almost everyone is on a fixed-term contract.

Worth asking about

For many fixed-term research contracts in Germany, the law (the Wissenschaftszeitvertragsgesetz, WissZeitVG) provides for the contract to be extended by periods of parental leave and maternity protection, and raises the maximum fixed-term period for people caring for children by two years per child.

Whether and how that applies to your contract (university, Max Planck, third-party funding or a stipend) is a question for your HR department or family office. It is also exactly what the guide below should answer.

From where you stand

Finally, we asked everyone how manageable it seems to combine an academic career in AI with raising children, from 1 (“very hard”) to 5 (“very manageable”). Twenty-five of the 47 chose 1 or 2, and nine chose 4 or 5. Only one person chose 5, and that person is a parent.

On average the parents were a little more optimistic than everyone else: 2.9 against 2.3, if one is allowed to average a five-point scale. With nine parents, that difference could easily be noise.

What would help

What could the Tübingen AI community do? Nobody chose “nothing needed”. The clear favourite was the most practical one: a guide to Kita, Elterngeld, contracts and paperwork, picked by 29 of the 41 who answered. Next came a babysitting or childcare-sharing network (23) and family-friendly events held in the daytime, with kids welcome (20). A parents' meetup or Slack channel got six votes, half of them from parents, who are the people it would be for.

We also asked for one piece of advice, or one question, about parenthood and an academic career. Here is a selection, sorted into four threads. We have lightly corrected spelling, and we do not say who wrote what.

01 When is the right time?

The question asked most often has no good answer. One parent answered it anyway.

“Is there a best time point to have kids in an academic career, or not really?”

“I'd love to know when in the academic path is best to have kids. Cause right now it looks like the wheel never stops.”

“Be brave! Not all of your plans will work out, but delaying plans and goals indefinitely for ‘when the time will be right’ is the most likely way of never realizing them. You can't necessarily control whether you'll get that professorship or tenure, and you can't control what you'll experience along the way, but you can decide whether to become a parent and when — and kids will teach you more flexibility than academia ever could!”

02 Fix the structure

Several answers rejected the premise that a meetup could solve this. They asked for money, contracts and childcare instead.

“Are you kidding me with these options?! More money and less stress and uncertainty is the only real answer.”

“Better salaries and contract conditions? An academic career is not a joke, and a PhD ‘student’ is only a ‘student’ on paper. We work at, and in many cases above, the level of experts in industry. I think it's high time that higher institutions stop treating early-career academics as free(-ish) labour.”

“On-site and emergency childcare is not really existent / long procedure / hard to come by. If the AI Center cares about retaining or getting more primary caregivers in research positions, they should make arrangements about childcare.”

“Pay for social freezing.”

03 Not the same for everyone

The gender gap in the numbers above did not go unnoticed by the people living it.

“There have been studies that male professors profit from having children (they might seem more approachable), while female professors get penalized for it (they don't have time for work anymore). It would be interesting to understand whether this also happens at an earlier level. For example, I know several male colleagues who have or are having kids, but none of my female colleagues have kids. Why do the women decide to postpone while the men don't?”

“Being a woman and wanting to have kids, I would love to know what women with kids in academia did to balance it and the work, as usually the mother has more responsibilities, or feels more responsible.”

“How can I best deal with the expected loss in performance?”

04 And what we didn't ask

One answer pointed out that infertility and fertility treatment are almost never part of this conversation. Others reminded us that not everyone wants children, and that is fine.

“Factor in that the decision to have kids and ultimately being able to have kids are two separate things. Ideally, academic structures would support both founding a family and employees who are facing uncertainty about whether they could or will become parents.”

“Doing two things that are terribly time-consuming and require you to commit your life to them is probably hard to do.”

“No advice or question, but thank you for considering this topic. This is a non-trivial and very important (for many) topic. And thank you for being respectful of those who may not want children, too!”

Inside · Non-profit lab

Inside KE:SAI

No investors, no equity, no patents. Tübingen's newest AI lab is trying to reach the frontier of self-driving with a fortieth of the data, and to give away everything it builds.

Interview by Haiwen Huang ·

First cut, about 2,100 words, down from about 3,700 in the Notion draft (tracker target: 800–1,200). Every theme is kept, with answers trimmed and some merged; wording stays the interviewees' own except for joins. Needs Andreas's sign-off before release, and a “how to get involved” box at the end.

KE:SAI (Kyutai ELLIS Scalable Autonomous Intelligence) builds world models, simulation and self-driving policies, and releases them openly. It is funded by donations; there is no equity in it and nothing to sell.

Over the summer I interviewed three of its founders separately. Andreas Geiger holds the chair for Autonomous Vision at the University of Tübingen and is on leave to serve as CEO. Kashyap Chitta did his PhD in the same group, spent a year at NVIDIA's autonomous-driving research group and returned as CTO. Bernhard Jaeger finished his PhD this year. Their answers are grouped by theme below.

“We believe something like the Linux of self-driving has to be established.”

The mission

What gap is KE:SAI trying to fill?

Geiger: In our field there's academic research, and there's frontier-AI-lab research, and there's very little connection in between. The frontier is driven primarily by a few siloed labs, and as the labs get bigger, they become less and less open. I'm fundamentally convinced that to solve these really challenging problems, we need the whole community. And in Europe we have the additional problem that we are behind. The European economy becomes more and more dependent on external AI suppliers from China and the US, despite the fact that we have the talent in Europe.

Look at the most popular operating system today: it thrived through the open-source community. So we believe something like the Linux of self-driving has to be established. There's Autoware, and there's comma.ai, but toward real frontier self-driving policies there's nothing open.

The best published result for long-range urban driving without intervention needed around 80,000 hours of proprietary data. KE:SAI has roughly 2,000 hours of open data. Is closing that gap the bet?

Geiger: I think so. Even with the enormous resources the big labs are spending, they still have a hard time generalizing to the long tail. Humans are orders of magnitude more efficient. And I think it's healthy to have a little bit of a bottleneck. We have much more compute than a university group, but much less than Wayve, Waymo and so on. Interesting research has to happen to reach the same performance with much less data and compute. And once we achieve it, we can probably scale the technology much more easily.

How it started

KE:SAI was announced in May 2026. When did it actually begin?

Jaeger: Two things came together. One was technological: in self-play simulation you can train with trillions of samples in a week, which made it plausible that you could build a self-driving car with much less funding than the big labs have. The other was the need. I try to do things that are needed but that others don't want to do: doing things openly, including the research.

Geiger: We had already drafted a first proposal for a non-profit, in particular with Bernhard Jaeger. We just weren't sure there would be any donors interested. Then we were in a meeting with board members from kyutai, an open-science lab in France that is well funded through private donors, and it turned out they wanted to expand from audio to physical AI and world models, and to Germany in particular. There's always a lot of talk about the Franco-German partnership, yet relatively little actually happens. So we said: why don't we just do something together? And they said: if you commit fully, we do this together and give you initial funding.

How did the plan change once the money was there?

Jaeger: What people told me at the start was: what you're asking for is the perfect research lab with no constraints. That's what every scientist wants and nobody gets. And why would they give it to a recently graduated PhD student? So the initial plan had to be extremely focused: one branch, and go all in on it. Andreas's strategy got us more funding than I thought we would get, then we asked Kashyap, and we diversified. Now if one branch fails, we still have plan B and plan C.

Chitta: I had planned to be involved as an advisor. When more funding was available, the plan could broaden: PhD positions, because motivated junior researchers with diverse backgrounds keep us learning, and a scope that grew to world modeling for physical AI, with autonomous driving as the primary application but not the only one. That is the version I decided to help build as a co-founder.

“Why would they give it to a recently graduated PhD student?”
“There's always a lot of talk about the Franco-German partnership, yet relatively little actually happens.”

The research

Where does an efficiency gain of that size actually come from?

Chitta: There is no existing approach that gets us the gains we need; the research has to happen here. But there are three initial directions. First, simulation. Simulators turn compute into data. We aim to make them more useful and, at the same time, less compute-intensive. Those two usually pull against each other, which is exactly why it is research. Second, active on-policy data is worth far more per hour than passive logged data. Recorded human driving almost never shows a mistake being corrected; data collected by a policy while it is learning contains recovery. Third, inductive biases, introduced through the data pipeline rather than the architecture: you stage the training, with different types of data at different stages, analogous to how large language model pipelines are built.

And what is being built right now?

Jaeger: The first step is a very strong reinforcement-learning teacher model in self-play simulation; that's what I'm focusing on. Then we distill that teacher, which works on ground-truth perception, into an end-to-end model that works from sensor inputs, and do sim-to-real transfer to the real car. Right now everything is tested on driving. We say “physical AI” because the methods are generic, but driving is hard enough that we get a good signal.

Geiger: The research vehicle that arrived in July is for small-scale data collection and closed-loop testing. We want to do 99% in simulation, but we always need real-world feedback, through our own car and through companies that give us access to their fleets. The first target is 30 kilometres between infractions, which the top labs can now achieve relatively easily. For us it's a new sensor setup, with much less data.

Why not a university, and why not a company

What can this lab do that a university group cannot?

Geiger: Compute, of course. The university has a big cluster, but so many users that in the end you don't get the compute. Then, the classical PhD is very focused on the six-month publication cycle. Frontier AI needs multiple people, very focused, on something where publication is not the primary focus. The primary focus is to advance the frontier, and the publication is a byproduct. And at the university the postdoc salary is barely more than the PhD salary. We can't offer what US tech companies offer, but we are competitive enough that a larger fraction of our team can be permanent staff, including people dedicated to the hardware or the cluster.

And why not a for-profit?

Jaeger: There are over a hundred companies working for-profit on self-driving. If I had made a new startup, I would have been the 101st, competing with Waymo and Tesla and already 10 to 15 years behind. If we want to achieve something that others haven't been able to achieve, we need to do something different, and that has to start at the structural level. As a non-profit, there's basically no competition. And it's okay to be late. DeepSeek gave the same performance as GPT-4 about a year later, and it was a big revolution because it was open, because it was free. If we are two years behind and we open something with Tesla-level performance, that still creates tremendous value.

“The primary focus is to advance the frontier, and the publication is a byproduct.”
“If I had made a new startup, I would have been the 101st.”

What “open” actually means

Economically, why would giving everything away work?

Chitta: End-to-end driving policies are extraordinarily expensive to train, which is why I think the best of them are closed. But a large part of the industry stands to gain immensely from a neutral, open stack. As we see with LLMs, smaller companies can build on open research instead of rebuilding it from nothing. And the team who did the original work can commercialize an open stack by delivering the most efficient implementation of it. That is an advantage you have to keep earning, which I think is healthy. So openness here is an accelerant.

Who is the open stack for, once it exists?

Geiger: It democratizes the technology. Robotaxis are more and more available, but there is no robotaxi in India, and the largest number of accidents and fatalities happens in India. And there are 200 car manufacturers, of which only a small fraction actually benefit from self-driving technology.

Jaeger: All the good self-driving software is in extremely premium cars. One hope I have is that open-sourcing gets this technology into lower-end cars as well.

Chitta: There is also a technical reason: if you want something that generalizes, you need data from places where traffic behaves differently.

The first months

What has founding an institution actually consumed?

Geiger: Mostly I have one hour to do actual work, and the remaining seven hours are the rest. It took us six weeks to set up a bank account: maybe fifty emails and ten meetings, just for a bank account. Then insurance, the legal structure, audits. A non-profit is much more complex than a standard for-profit startup. And hiring is very time-intensive: if you just do interviews for three months, you don't get any work done.

Update, October: “This has changed now. I do 50/50, and I actually have much more time to work on the methods myself, which is a lot of fun.”

Jaeger: My first three months went mostly into the compute cluster. We rent compute for this year, because building up a cluster takes time, and for next year we've ordered GPUs to co-locate in a data centre. Now that's over, and we've done some hiring, so I have much more time for actual research.

Chitta: There are around ten projects running, and much of my job is to be the bridge between them. I'm also starting as a Principal Investigator at ELLIS, where the world models developed at KE:SAI can be tested on new domains.

“It took us six weeks to set up a bank account.”
“There is no robotaxi in India.”

What comes next

What does the coming year look like?

Geiger: Collaborations with external partners that we've started recently: NVIDIA, Valeo and NYU, among others. They have their own engineers and fleets, so we get feedback on what needs to change in our policies. And along the way we want to raise more funding, so we can expand.

The lab is built around a CEO who has said he will eventually step back. What does that do to it?

Geiger: It's not completely determined how long I stay full-time. At least a year, maybe five. Once the organisation runs smoothly, an external hire can take care of the administrative side, and my role would be more on the scientific side again. I'll always be involved, at least 20%. At the moment I see myself as a catalyst.

Jaeger: What matters is that Andreas is here right now as a full-time CEO. Without him we wouldn't be able to hire this world-class talent. A year from now, at 20%, it doesn't risk the life of KE:SAI.

And if it works? What happens to the technology then?

Geiger: Even when the technology is ripe, there's still a large gap from research to commercialization: legislation, depots, maintenance, ride-hailing apps. That could be done by companies in-house, or by a translational for-profit spun off from us. kyutai, our mother company in a way, has already done this: they developed audio technology as an open-science lab, then spun off Gradium late last year, with a $70 million seed round.

Jaeger: The important part is that KE:SAI doesn't own IP. We don't have patents. We open-source the software, so anybody can do the same thing.

And the lab itself, in that scenario?

Geiger: The research entity should still exist, because it needs to focus on the next research challenges. There is a large number of important problems that society has to tackle in the next decades. Research organizations like ours are ideally suited to do so.

These interviews were conducted separately in Tübingen in summer 2026, with an update in October. They have been edited and condensed for clarity.

▶ LLM providers hide their models' chain of thought, returning it to the client as encrypted blocks that are passed back with each request. These blocks turn out to be interchangeable across sessions, users and models of the same provider. Injecting a strong model's encrypted trace into a weaker, less safeguarded model makes the weaker one decode it verbatim, without ever jailbreaking the stronger model.
18 Sep 2026 · Alexander Panfilov

Stolen Thoughts: Stealing Reasoning Traces from Proprietary LLM APIs

Encrypted reasoning traces are interchangeable across models, so a weaker model can be made to decrypt a stronger one's.

▶ LLM-as-a-judge is cheaper than human annotation, but position bias, self-preference and intransitivity miscalibrate the rankings. Propagating calibrated win probabilities into Bradley–Terry brings LLM-derived ratings within 17.9 Elo of human ones across 55 held-out models on LMArena, and split conformal prediction adds intervals with distribution-free coverage guarantees.
18 Sep 2026 · Bora Kargi

Conformal Elo Estimation for LLM Evaluation

Calibrated LLM-judge ratings within 18 Elo of human ones, with honest uncertainty bounds.

→ Watch on YouTube
▶ Classical track fitting at the Large Hadron Collider uses Kalman filters and least-squares fits, reaching a precision of one part in 10⁵. Treating charged-particle trajectory regression as sequence modelling, a bidirectional gated linear recurrent encoder reproduces that full precision, and with a custom kernel runs significantly faster on GPUs than classical software on similarly priced CPU servers.

Learned Bidirectional State-Space Models for High-Precision Track Fitting at the LHC

A recurrent sequence model matches classical track fitting to one part in 10⁵, and runs faster.

Committed Q-learning, a variant whose behaviour policy commits to one action until the observed feature changes, provably converges to the optimal reactive policy under a condition strictly weaker than those used in prior work.

Q-learning that commits to an action until the observation changes, with convergence guarantees beyond the Markov assumption.

Binary conditioning trees recursively partition a population into subgroups and reveal a “macro fallacy”: estimates rebuilt from subgroup predictions are often more accurate than direct population-level ones. Label-free self-consistency checks show such violations remain widespread across frontier models.

Partition, Prompt, Aggregate: Statistical Self-Consistency in Language Models

Asking an LLM about subgroups and adding up often beats asking about the whole population directly.

Christmas Markets: Where They Are, and How They Are

Four markets within easy reach of the campus, from the region's medieval favourite to a one-weekend wonder in the old town. Where to go, what to expect, and when to stay away.

By Fynn Neurath · Dates for 2026
26 Nov – 22 Dec

Reutlingen Weihnachtsmarkt

I have never visited this one myself: as a good Tübingen citizen, I categorically avoided Reutlingen whenever possible. The market also has a new main organiser this year, so some things will probably be different from last year. Might be worth checking out!

No website found yet

24 Nov – 22 Dec

Esslingen Weihnachtsmarkt

For many people in this region, this is the go-to Christmas market, and for good reason. It's pretty big, has a medieval-themed section and offers some events. If you feel like it, bring your swimwear for some public hot-tub bathing (you need to book that). Being the go-to choice also means it's crowded, especially on weekend evenings, so be prepared.

→ esslingen-info.com

11 – 13 Dec

Tübingen Weihnachtsmarkt

More of a one-weekend wonder. It's not as big as the market in Esslingen or the chocolART, and if you look the wrong way for a moment, you could miss it. But it has some cute booths, many of them run by local businesses, and some offer vegan Bratwurst.

→ tuemarkt.de

1 – 6 Dec

Tübingen chocolART

Tübingen's main Christmas event, and all about chocolate: every kind of chocolate and chocolate product, chocolate liqueur to get drunk on, chocolate art to buy. What's true for Esslingen is doubly true here: it's famous, so it's packed. If you'd like to experience the hypothetical emotions of a water molecule in a clogged garden hose, a Saturday evening at the chocolART will get you pretty close. If you can manage to go earlier in the week, it's okay…

→ krone-tuebingen.de
