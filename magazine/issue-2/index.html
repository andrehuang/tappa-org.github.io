Welcome back to TAPPAS.

This issue has two headline stories. The first is the one we promised at the end of Issue 01: we go inside KE:SAI, the Tübingen non-profit that wants to build the Linux of self-driving, with no investors, no equity and no patents, and that plans to give away everything it builds.

The second is about a question many of us think about and rarely talk about at work: whether, and when, to have children. This autumn we asked, and 47 of you answered. The results are in By the Numbers. Most of us who don't have children would like to. Most of those are waiting. And almost nobody knows what our workplaces actually offer. Alongside the numbers, People talks to parents in Tübingen's AI labs about what really changes.

Since it is winter, Fynn has sorted the region's Christmas markets so you can pick the right one, and the right night, and Rabanus has the dates. Rabanus also makes the case for joining a Verein, in a new Culture section. As before, everything here was written, surveyed and photographed by people from our community, and we would love more of you on these pages: a piece, an interview, a photo, a rant. Find us in #tappas on Slack, or through the feedback form at the back.

Survey

Parenthood and AI Careers

Whether and when to have children is something many of us think about and rarely talk about at work. So we asked, and 47 of you answered. Most of those without children want them. Most of those are waiting. And almost nobody knows what their workplace offers.

By Haiwen Huang & Mina Remeli ·

At the start of November we sent round a short survey, Parenthood and AI Careers. It asked parents what changed and what helped. It asked everyone else whether they want children and what holds them back. And it asked all of us what our workplaces offer, and what this community could do.

Forty-seven of you answered: nine parents (one of them expecting), 37 people without children, and one person who preferred not to say. As with the flying survey in Issue 01, this is not a representative sample. People who care about the topic are more likely to answer, and nine parents are too few to generalise from. That is why we give counts rather than percentages. But the pattern in the answers is clear enough to be worth reading.

Thank you to everyone who took the five minutes, and especially to those who wrote something in the free-text boxes. A selection of those answers closes this piece.

The survey, in brief

47 answers from the Tübingen AI community, collected between late October and 10 November 2026. Anonymous, voluntary, and not representative. 27 women and 20 men answered.

Wanting, and waiting

Of the 37 respondents without children, 18 said they would like to have children someday and 14 that they might. Four said no, and three of those four added that their career has nothing to do with it. That is worth saying out loud: not wanting children is a complete answer, not a symptom of a bad job.

For the other 32, the career is very much part of it. Seventeen said their academic career has made them postpone, or think about postponing, having children significantly; eleven somewhat. Only one said no.

The difference between women and men is one of degree, not direction. Almost everyone who wants children has felt the pull to wait: 18 of 21 women, and 10 of 11 men. But 13 of the 21 women said significantly, against 4 of the 11 men.

“Right now it looks like the wheel never stops.”
— from the survey's free-text answers

What holds us back

We asked those 32 to pick their top three concerns. The answers are about the shape of an academic career far more than about babies. Falling behind on publications came first (15 of the 31 who answered), then fixed-term contracts and having to move for the next position (14), and salary (12). Age and biological timing also drew 12, and here the gender gap is wide: 10 of the 20 women who answered picked it, against 2 of the 11 men.

One option nobody picked is worth noting. Not a single person chose “how supervisors or colleagues would react”. Finding childcare was picked once, though the parents, as we will see, tell a different story. The worry, in other words, is not the reaction in the room. It is the contract, the CV and the clock.

“The need to publish and have a good career, so as to have stability and the possibility of having kids — which itself makes the ‘falling behind’ point happen.”
— a concern, in one respondent's own words

What the parents told us

Nine respondents are parents or expecting. Four became parents during their PhD, three as postdocs (one of them is still expecting) and two outside academia. For their most recent child they took, or plan to take, anything from no time off to a year: one took no leave, two less than two months, three between two and six months, and three between six and twelve.

Asked how supported they felt, the parents gave their supervisors and colleagues mostly good marks; five felt “very” supported by their supervisor. Their institutions got a lukewarm “fairly” from seven of the nine. Childcare split the group: four felt very supported by it, two only a little or not at all. One parent wrote that on-site and emergency childcare is “not really existent”.

And almost everything changed. Seven parents answered our checklist of what has changed since becoming a parent (one answered an earlier version of the form, and one is still expecting). All seven ticked when and how they organise their work, and how much they travel to conferences. Six ticked how many hours they work and what they prioritise. Four ticked their career plans, including whether to stay in academia. Nobody ticked “nothing major has changed”.

The most common answer: “Don't know”

We asked everyone whether, as far as they know, their workplace offers five kinds of support. Only one is widely known: 32 of 47 said they can get flexible hours or remote work. For everything else “don't know” dominated: 25 for Kita places, 31 for a family office or contact point, 35 for emergency childcare, and 36 for contract extensions. Eight people answered “don't know” to all five.

Part of this is structural. Tübingen's AI community works for many employers (the university, Max Planck, ELLIS, Hertie, companies), each with its own offers, which is also why the same question drew both “yes” and “no”: eleven of each for Kita places. But it is not just newcomers who don't know. Eight of the nine parents did not know whether fixed-term contracts can be extended for parents. That is the question with the most at stake, for a community in which almost everyone is on a fixed-term contract.

Worth asking about

For many fixed-term research contracts in Germany, the law (the Wissenschaftszeitvertragsgesetz, WissZeitVG) provides for the contract to be extended by periods of parental leave and maternity protection, and raises the maximum fixed-term period for people caring for children by two years per child.

Whether and how that applies to your contract (university, Max Planck, third-party funding or a stipend) is a question for your HR department or family office. It is also exactly what the guide below should answer.

From where you stand

Finally, we asked everyone how manageable it seems to combine an academic career in AI with raising children, from 1 (“very hard”) to 5 (“very manageable”). Twenty-five of the 47 chose 1 or 2, and nine chose 4 or 5. Only one person chose 5, and that person is a parent.

On average the parents were a little more optimistic than everyone else: 2.9 against 2.3, if one is allowed to average a five-point scale. With nine parents, that difference could easily be noise.

What would help

What could the Tübingen AI community do? Nobody chose “nothing needed”. The clear favourite was the most practical one: a guide to Kita, Elterngeld, contracts and paperwork, picked by 29 of the 41 who answered. Next came a babysitting or childcare-sharing network (23) and family-friendly events held in the daytime, with kids welcome (20). A parents' meetup or Slack channel got six votes, half of them from parents, who are the people it would be for.

We also asked for one piece of advice, or one question, about parenthood and an academic career. Here is a selection, sorted into four threads. We have lightly corrected spelling, and we do not say who wrote what.

01 When is the right time?

The question asked most often has no good answer. One parent answered it anyway.

“Is there a best time point to have kids in an academic career, or not really?”

“I'd love to know when in the academic path is best to have kids. Cause right now it looks like the wheel never stops.”

“Be brave! Not all of your plans will work out, but delaying plans and goals indefinitely for ‘when the time will be right’ is the most likely way of never realizing them. You can't necessarily control whether you'll get that professorship or tenure, and you can't control what you'll experience along the way, but you can decide whether to become a parent and when — and kids will teach you more flexibility than academia ever could!”

02 Fix the structure

Several answers rejected the premise that a meetup could solve this. They asked for money, contracts and childcare instead.

“Are you kidding me with these options?! More money and less stress and uncertainty is the only real answer.”

“Better salaries and contract conditions? An academic career is not a joke, and a PhD ‘student’ is only a ‘student’ on paper. We work at, and in many cases above, the level of experts in industry. I think it's high time that higher institutions stop treating early-career academics as free(-ish) labour.”

“On-site and emergency childcare is not really existent / long procedure / hard to come by. If the AI Center cares about retaining or getting more primary caregivers in research positions, they should make arrangements about childcare.”

“Pay for social freezing.”

03 Not the same for everyone

The gender gap in the numbers above did not go unnoticed by the people living it.

“There have been studies that male professors profit from having children (they might seem more approachable), while female professors get penalized for it (they don't have time for work anymore). It would be interesting to understand whether this also happens at an earlier level. For example, I know several male colleagues who have or are having kids, but none of my female colleagues have kids. Why do the women decide to postpone while the men don't?”

“Being a woman and wanting to have kids, I would love to know what women with kids in academia did to balance it and the work, as usually the mother has more responsibilities, or feels more responsible.”

“How can I best deal with the expected loss in performance?”

04 And what we didn't ask

One answer pointed out that infertility and fertility treatment are almost never part of this conversation. Others reminded us that not everyone wants children, and that is fine.

“Factor in that the decision to have kids and ultimately being able to have kids are two separate things. Ideally, academic structures would support both founding a family and employees who are facing uncertainty about whether they could or will become parents.”

“Doing two things that are terribly time-consuming and require you to commit your life to them is probably hard to do.”

“No advice or question, but thank you for considering this topic. This is a non-trivial and very important (for many) topic. And thank you for being respectful of those who may not want children, too!”

Inside · Non-profit lab

Inside KE:SAI

No investors, no equity, no patents. Tübingen's newest AI lab is trying to reach the frontier of self-driving with a fortieth of the data — and to give away everything it builds.

Interview by Haiwen Huang ·

Restored from the Notion draft with light trims: about 3,400 words, down from about 3,750 (tracker target: 800–1,200). All 23 questions are kept; cuts are repetitions, side remarks and filler inside answers, and the wording is the interviewees' own. One wording fix to check with Andreas: “recognised as an official research and academic institution” (transcript: “granted as”). Needs Andreas's sign-off before release, and a “how to get involved” box at the end.

KE:SAI — Kyutai ELLIS Scalable Autonomous Intelligence — builds world models, simulation and self-driving policies, and releases them openly. It is funded by donations, and there is no equity in it and nothing to sell.

Over the summer I interviewed three of its founders separately: Andreas Geiger, who holds the chair for Autonomous Vision at the University of Tübingen and is on leave to serve as CEO; Kashyap Chitta, who finished his PhD in the same group, spent a year at NVIDIA's autonomous-driving research group and returned as CTO; and Bernhard Jaeger, who finished his PhD this year. Their answers are grouped by theme below.

KE:SAI in brief

“We believe something like the Linux of self-driving has to be established.”

The mission

What gap is KE:SAI trying to fill?

Geiger: In our field there's academic research, and there's frontier-AI-lab research, and there's very little connection in between. The community is huge, but the frontier is driven primarily by a few siloed labs — and as the labs get bigger, they become less and less open. I'm fundamentally convinced that to solve these really challenging problems, we need the whole community.

And in Europe we have the additional problem that we are behind. The European economy becomes more and more dependent on external AI suppliers from China and the US — despite the fact that we have the talent in Europe. And all the science and research in Europe depends on the welfare of the economy, because it's financed through taxes. So it's not just the companies that need to survive; the whole economy needs to survive.

Look at the most popular operating system today: it thrived through the open-source community. So we believe something like the Linux of self-driving has to be established, and we want to drive that progress together with the community. There's Autoware, and there's comma.ai, but toward real frontier self-driving policies there's nothing open.

The best published result for long-range urban driving without intervention needed around 80,000 hours of proprietary data, and the model can't be released. KE:SAI has roughly 2,000 hours of open data. Is closing that gap the bet?

Geiger: I think so. Even with these enormous resources the big labs are spending, they still have a hard time generalizing to the long tail. And it's even worse: only a handful of labs have the financial resources to do this very large-scale training. Humans are orders of magnitude more efficient.

And I think it's healthy to have a little bit of a bottleneck. We are not as bottlenecked as the university, where we couldn't do this type of research at all — we have much more compute. But we have much less compute than Wayve, Waymo and so on. We try to achieve some of the same performance with much less data and compute, and interesting research has to happen to make that possible. Once we achieve it, we can probably scale the technology much more easily.

How it started

KE:SAI was announced in May 2026. When did it actually begin?

Jaeger: Two things came together. One was technological: there were developments that made it plausible that you could build a self-driving car with much less funding than the big labs have — so that you could actually do this in a non-profit environment. In self-play simulation you can train with trillions of samples in a week, which is not possible with real data.

The other was the need. I try to do things that are needed but that others don't want to do: doing things openly, including the research. This is especially the case for autonomous driving.

Geiger: We had already had longer discussions, in particular with Bernhard Jaeger, on establishing a non-profit to do research in this space, because there's so much closed research happening in physical AI. We had already drafted a first proposal. We just weren't sure there would be any donors interested in supporting it.

Was there resistance to the idea?

Jaeger: Yes, initially there were concerns about technological feasibility and funding. But two things convinced us to go ahead anyway. One was stubbornness — okay, I'm going to do this; if I fail, I fail. The other was that we found other organisations like us. We didn't know they existed, but there are a few around the world. In the US they're called Focused Research Organizations — a similar non-profit status, funded by donations from wealthy people, with this open-science mindset. And they were making the same arguments: that there are gaps in science that universities can't address and industry doesn't want to address because it's closed.

And how did it finally become a feasible plan?

Geiger: We were in a meeting with board members from kyutai — an open-science lab in France, well funded through private donors — and it turned out they wanted to expand from their audio background to physical AI and world models, and to Germany in particular. There's always a lot of talk about the Franco-German partnership, yet relatively little actually happens; Europe stays very fragmented. We think the best way to solve this is not top-down but bottom-up. So we said: why don't we just do something together? And they said: if you commit fully, we do this together and give you initial funding.

How did the plan change once the money was there?

Jaeger: What people told me at the start was: the thing you're asking for is the perfect research lab that has no constraints — as much funding as possible, no bureaucracy. That's what every scientist wants and nobody gets. And why would they give it to a recently graduated PhD student?

So the initial plan had to be extremely focused, just to have a chance at enough money. That changed once Andreas came. His strategy got us more funding than I thought we would get, and then we asked Kashyap, and we diversified. In the beginning we had one branch that might succeed, and the plan was to go all in on it. Now we have several, so if one fails we still have plan B and plan C.

Chitta: I planned to be involved as an advisor initially. I wanted to continue my postdoc at NVIDIA and look for longer-term academic positions, and I thought advising was a better fit than being part of the founding team. The first version of the plan was narrow — a five-year push at a specific, promising solution, with very little room to explore new frontiers.

When more funding was available, the plan could broaden. By February 2026 we had discussed the inclusion of PhD positions. I thought that having some motivated junior researchers with diverse backgrounds as part of the team would be critical. It would keep us learning, help train a new generation of experts, and increase the chances we make major breakthroughs. The research scope could also broaden to general world modeling for physical AI, with autonomous driving as the primary application, but not the only one. This is the version I decided to help build as a co-founder rather than an advisor.

What they are working on

Where does an efficiency gain of that size actually come from?

Chitta: We will need to do the research here; there is no existing approach that gets us the gains we need. However, we are working on some initial directions.

First, we believe in simulation. We aim to make simulators more useful and, at the same time, less compute-intensive. Those two usually pull against each other, which is exactly why it is research. Simulators turn compute into data, which gives us a scalable way to increase data quantity while keeping costs low.

Second, we believe that active on-policy data is worth far more per hour than the passive logged data that most physical AI efforts are collecting today. Recorded human driving almost never shows a mistake being corrected, because the driver rarely makes one, and never deliberately. On-policy data collected by a policy while it is learning contains recovery.

The third is inductive biases during learning. We favor introducing these into the model through the data pipeline rather than through architectures. Instead of hand-designing structure, you stage the training — different types of data at different stages, analogous to how large language model pipelines are built. This can significantly improve data and compute efficiency.

And in practice, what is being built right now?

Jaeger: The first step is a very strong reinforcement-learning teacher model in self-play simulation — that's what I'm focusing on. Then we take that teacher, which is a privileged model working on ground-truth perception, and distill it into a full end-to-end model that works from sensor inputs. Once that's trained in simulation, we do sim-to-real transfer to the real car.

The lab talks about physical AI, but everything here is driving.

Jaeger: Right now all our research directions are tested on the autonomous driving task. We put “physical AI” on it because we work on generic methods — they can also be applied to other robots. But we don't have the capacity to try and test on ten different robotics tasks. Driving is hard enough that we get a good signal.

And the research vehicle that arrived in July?

Geiger: It's for small-scale data collection and closed-loop testing in the real world. We want to do 99% in simulation, but we always need the real-world feedback — through our own car, and through collaborations: a lot of companies are interested in giving us access to their fleet so we can test our algorithms on it, or giving us their data and opening it to the community together with us. The first target is a baseline of 30 kilometres between infractions, which the top labs can now achieve relatively easily. But for us, it's a new sensor setup, on which we're already a little out of domain, and with much less data.

Why not a university, and why not a company

What can this lab do that a university group cannot?

Geiger: Compute, of course — despite the university having a big cluster, there are so many users that in the end you don't get the compute.

Then, the classical PhD is very focused on the six-month publication cycle. That doesn't work so well with frontier AI, where you need multiple people, very focused, working on something where publication is not the primary focus — the primary focus is to advance the frontier, and the publication is a byproduct.

Third, at the university it's very difficult to hire postdocs, because the postdoc salary level is limited — barely more than the PhD salary. Most of the people we'd like to hire just prefer to go to US tech companies. Of course we don't provide the same benefits as they would get there, but we are competitive enough that a larger fraction of our team can be permanent staff rather than PhD students. It's also much easier for us to hire a scientific coordinator, or somebody dedicated to the hardware or the cluster, which we simply can't at the university.

And why not a for-profit? Why no compromise on the non-profit form?

Jaeger: Why would I go with a compromise? There are over a hundred companies working for-profit to develop self-driving technology. If I had made a new startup, I would have been the 101st company trying to do this. If we want to achieve something that others haven't been able to achieve, we need to do something different — and I think that has to start at the structural level of the company.

If I start a new company now, I have to compete with Waymo, with Tesla, and I'm already 10 to 15 years behind. If we start a non-profit, there's basically no competition.

An open lab still competes with closed ones.

Jaeger: Yes, but it's okay to be late. DeepSeek gave the same performance as GPT-4 about a year later. But it was a big revolution because it was open, because it was free. If we are two years behind and we open something with Tesla-level performance, that still creates tremendous value. Whereas if we build a closed project and we're two years behind, that's not going to create much value for humanity.

What does the non-profit status cost in practice?

Geiger: It gives us a five-year plan — a longer plan than a for-profit has. But we are tax-exempt and need to be very careful not to lose that status: any activity that unilaterally benefits one partner or one company does not agree with the non-profit status. We can collaborate with academic partners, and with industry partners, as long as they subscribe — for that part of the collaboration — to the open science we ask them to subscribe to. We're also recognised as an official research and academic institution now.

“We want to do 99% in simulation, but we always need the real-world feedback.”
“The primary focus is to advance the frontier, and the publication is a byproduct.”
“If I had made a new startup, I would have been the 101st company trying to do this.”

“If we are two years behind and we open something with Tesla-level performance, that still creates tremendous value.”

What “open” actually means

What does being open cost? Does it stop you doing anything?

Jaeger: For us, not really. We're funded by donations, so we don't need to make money. We can't work with some companies that want to keep everything secret. So we collaborate with the companies that are also doing open source, which is Valeo, NVIDIA and so on.

Economically, why would giving everything away work?

Chitta: End-to-end driving policies are extraordinarily expensive to train, which is why I think the best of them are closed. But there is a large proportion of the industry that stands to gain immensely from the availability of a neutral, open stack. Open-sourcing our findings, models and datasets changes who can participate. As we see with LLMs, smaller companies can build on open research instead of rebuilding it from nothing. And the team who did the original work can commercialize an open stack by being the ones who can deliver the most efficient, highest-throughput implementation of it. That is an advantage you have to keep earning, which I think is healthy.

So openness here is an accelerant. And it is not only about driving. If the tools are good enough to serve as a blueprint, other fields can pick them up — industrial robotics, construction, micromobility, and others.

Who is the open stack for, once it exists?

Geiger: It democratizes the technology. Think about robotaxis: they're more and more available, but there is no robotaxi in India — and the largest number of accidents and fatalities happens in India. The technology is unequally distributed over the world. And there are 200 car manufacturers, of which only a small fraction actually benefit from self-driving technology.

Jaeger: All the good self-driving software is in extremely premium cars. So one hope I have is that open-sourcing everything we can gets this technology into lower-end cars as well, because software has a much lower total cost of ownership.

Chitta: There is also a technical reason to care about the rest of the world, not only an ethical one: if you want something that generalizes, you need data from places where traffic behaves differently. Having an open stack incentivizes deployments in places which could provide data that is far more valuable than collecting more from existing deployments.

The first months

What has founding an institution actually consumed?

Geiger: Currently, I mostly have one hour to do actual work, and the remaining seven hours are the rest. It took us six weeks to set up a bank account — we wrote maybe fifty emails and had ten meetings, just to set up a bank account. And there are many of these things: insurance, all the legal stuff. A non-profit is also much more complex than a for-profit — you need audits and all the legal structure in place.

Hiring is very time-intensive. If you just do interviews for three months, you don't get any work done. So you need to think: do you want to hire, or do you want to work?

Update, October: “This has changed. I do 50/50 now. And I do have actually much more time to work on the methods myself, which is a lot of fun.”

Jaeger: The beginning was just organisational. I took responsibility for the compute cluster and the infrastructure. What we ended up doing is renting compute for a year — when you buy a cluster it takes time to build it up, so renting is the only way to get it fast. And for next year we've ordered GPUs to build our own and co-locate them in a data centre. It consumed most of my time in the first three months. But now that's over, and we've done some hiring, so I have much more time for actual research.

What does the structure look like inside?

Jaeger: We have a flat hierarchy. Everyone's opinion matters, from undergraduate intern to permanent staff. Important strategic decisions are made jointly by the co-founders.

Chitta: There are around ten projects running, and much of my job is to be the bridge between them. We generally collaborate with other groups doing great open science, and aim to publish longer-term, high-impact results. I am also starting as a Principal Investigator at ELLIS, where the world models developed at KE:SAI, and the technical know-how on how they are built, can be tested on new domains.

What comes next

What does the coming year look like concretely?

Geiger: In the collaborations with external partners that we have been starting recently — NVIDIA, Valeo and NYU, amongst others — they have their own engineers and AI teams. We can't put our technology on their fleet ourselves, so we rely on their people, but we get the feedback on what needs to change and how to improve our policies. And along the way we also want to collect more funding, so we can expand.

The lab is built around a CEO who has said he will eventually step back. What does that do to it?

Geiger: It's not completely determined how long I stay here full-time. At least a year, maybe five. Once the organisation runs smoothly, it's okay to have an external hire who primarily takes care of the administrative side, and my role would be more on the scientific side again — I'll always be involved, at least 20%, even in the long term. At the moment I see myself as a catalyst, to set it up.

Jaeger: The administrative work is going to wind down rapidly now. What matters is that Andreas is here right now as a full-time CEO — if we didn't have him, we wouldn't be able to hire this world-class talent. A year from now, at 20%, it doesn't risk the life of KE:SAI.

And if it works? Say the technology is there — what happens to it then?

Geiger: Even when the technology is ripe, there's still a large gap from research to commercialization. That could be done by the companies in-house, or by some translational company — a for-profit that takes our technology, which could be spun off from us, or an external entity we collaborate closely with. There are all these additional questions: legislation, and for fleets, depots, maintenance, ride-hailing apps. That's not our focus. Our focus is the core technology, pushing it as far as possible in the open space.

kyutai — our mother company, in a way — has already instantiated this. They call it the “fluid model.” They developed audio technology as an open-science lab, then spun off Gradium in November or December last year, with a $70 million seed round, to commercialize the technology. There's even the possibility for employees to work half-time in the for-profit and half-time in the non-profit.

Jaeger: The important part is that KE:SAI doesn't own IP. We have no legal advantage over anybody else doing this. We don't have patents. We open-source the software, so anybody can do the same thing.

And the lab itself, in that scenario?

Geiger: The research entity should still exist, because it needs to focus on the next research challenges. There is a large number of important problems that society has to tackle in the next decades. Research organizations like ours are ideally suited to do so.

Vereinsleben — “Club” Culture in Germany

Of all the stereotypes about Germans, “Germans are members of a club” may be the most accurate. Why a Verein is one of the best ways into life in Tübingen, and where to start.

By Rabanus Derr ·

Contributed by Rabanus Derr; text from his Notion page, lightly copy-edited for grammar and house style (e.g. “members of a club”, “600,000”, “German Alpine Club” for the DAV, British spelling). Rabanus to check. The standfirst is ours. The empty table at the end of the Notion page is left out.

When you arrive new to Germany, you might already know such stereotypes as “Germans are emotionally cold” or “Germans are punctual”. Surprisingly, however, there is a stereotype which is probably a lot more accurate than those: “Germans are members of a club”.

Vereine, German for “clubs”, are a central cornerstone of social life. There are Vereine for sports, music, gaming, animal breeding, cars, history, social support, mountaineering, you name it. In fact, so-called eingetragene Vereine (registered clubs) are legal entities comparable to companies. According to Wikipedia, there were about 600,000 Vereine in Germany in 2022. Are you already a member of one?

If you would like to get in touch with people inside and outside the Tübingen AI bubble, or with more local people, or you would just like to do some sport together, then look for a matching Verein! Vereine are a great opportunity to meet new people and to feel more at home in Tübingen.

Becoming a member of a Verein is usually rather cheap. Most often you pay a yearly fee. Still, you get to use gyms, instruments and trainers. How does that work? Through voluntary work. Vereine live because being a member is a relationship of giving and taking. For instance, you take volleyball training, and you give cake on a match day. That is a double plus: enjoy playing volleyball together, and enjoy eating cake ;)

To make the first step easier, we have a short (non-exhaustive) list of different clubs for you below! Take the first step: check out when they meet and what they do, get in touch and try it out. Checking out a club meeting usually costs nothing except working up the courage.

Three words to know
Verein
A club or association. Plural: Vereine.
e.V.
eingetragener Verein, a registered club: a legal entity comparable to a company.
Vereinsleben
Club life, and the social life that happens around it.
“Checking out a club meeting usually costs nothing except working up the courage.”

Where to start

Sports clubs

Tübingen has three major sports clubs: TSG Tübingen, SV 03 Tübingen and SSC Tübingen. They offer sports like basketball, floorball, lacrosse, American football, tennis, Ultimate Frisbee, dancing, cheerleading, volleyball and, of course, football.

But there are a lot more than those three: sfs-tuebingen.de has a more detailed list of sports clubs. Almost every part of town has its own sports club, e.g. Lustnau, Pfrondorf, Derendingen and Hagelloch. So you will find something close to where you live!

Mountaineering

The intersection between computer scientists and bouldering people is extremely non-empty :D Hence, many of you might already know the DAV (German Alpine Club). This club is more than the B12 bouldering gym: it offers courses in mountaineering, skiing and climbing, for almost all levels.

Music

You play an instrument, but without a band or an orchestra your trombone doesn't sound as nice? There are orchestras, choirs, bands… e.g. the Freie Sinfonie Tübingen orchestra or the KHG choir. Surely there are more (see the list below).

Other clubs of interest

Elysium Gaming is the first gaming club in the region. The Chaostreff Tübingen e.V. organises the Digital Freedom Days as part of the larger Chaos Computer Club, which is “Europe's largest association of hackers”. And of course there is also a punting club: the Stocherkahnverein Tübingen e.V.

For a longer list of Vereine in Tübingen: tuebingen.de/vereine (in German).
